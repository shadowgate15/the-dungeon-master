# DMG Ch 1–2 on building Worlds: a gap analysis

Date: 2026-11-21. Source: Dungeon Master's Guide (5e, 2014), Chapter 1 "A World of Your Own" (pp. 9–41) and Chapter 2 "Creating a Multiverse" (pp. 43–68). Ticket: #279 (map #277).

Everything about the book below is a paraphrase with page references. The book is copyrighted, so nothing here quotes its text or reproduces its tables. Shipped mechanics still come from the SRD 5.1.

## Question

What does the DMG recommend for building a World: gods and pantheons, factions, settlements, history, magic, cosmology and themes? How does that compare with what we have (the World, its Seed and Seed card, and World memory with its Places, NPCs, Facts and the deferred factions)? Which changes are candidates?

## Answer

The book treats a world as more than its premise. It is a set of answered questions: who the gods are, who holds power, what happened before, how common magic is, and what the stakes are at each level of play. The DM answers them **small and local first**, and the world grows outward as play needs it. Our World is a name, a premise and four enums. **The Narrator sees none of it**: its fixed context is the Campaign Seed and the Character sheet. The Arc and first-scene prompts see only the World's name and premise, and the genre, scale, magic and tech fields never reach any prompt.

The most valuable changes in order:

1. Get the World we already store in front of the Narrator.
2. Give the World a little authored lore: faiths, powers and factions, history, magic norms and naming.
3. Give each Campaign a home base and a starting region.
4. Plan world-shaking events on the Arc.

Cosmology, calendars and renown matter less to a solo, low-level, text-first Session. They come later. The full numbered list is under [Candidate changes](#candidate-changes).

## What the book recommends

### Start from assumptions, then bend them (pp. 9–10)

The book states the default assumptions of a D&D world:

- The gods are real and act.
- Most of the world is untamed wilderness.
- The world is old, with ruined empires under the present.
- Conflict between factions drives history.
- Magic is uncommon in daily life, but its traces are everywhere.

The suggested way to make a world your own is to ask "what if" one of these were different. The world might be mundane, newly made or fully mapped. Monsters might be rare, magic everywhere, or the gods might be absent or walking the earth. One deliberate inversion gives a setting its identity.

### Gods and faith (pp. 10–13)

- **Loose pantheon (the default).** Many gods, each with a portfolio, sometimes with an alignment and a domain. Ordinary people honor several gods depending on need. Temples and shrines are civic hubs. A quick way to build one is a god per cleric domain. The book offers a sample pantheon (the "Dawn War" gods) as a table.
- **Other faith structures:**
  - A tight pantheon: a few gods sharing one ethos, plus outcast gods that people fear.
  - Mystery cults: initiation re-enacts a god's myth.
  - Monotheism: one god with many aspects.
  - Dualism: two opposed forces.
  - Animism: spirits in places and things.
  - Impersonal forces or philosophies that grant power without a god.
- **Divine rank.** Greater gods, lesser gods and quasi-deities: demigods, titans, vestiges.
- **Peoples and their gods.** Some peoples have a single creator god. Human faith varies by culture. Prophets found religions, and dynasties claim divine favor.

### Mapping (p. 14)

You can build top-down (continent first) or bottom-up (start village first). Maps come in three nested scales: province, kingdom and continent. *Maps are a README "Later" item, so this is out of scope apart from the idea of nesting the scales.*

### Settlements (pp. 15–21)

- **Questions to answer about any settlement:**
  - What is it for in the story?
  - How big is it, and who lives there?
  - What does it look, sound and smell like?
  - Who rules it, officially and in fact?
  - How is it defended?
  - What does it trade?
  - Which temples and organizations operate there?
  - What fantastic element does it have?
  - Why do the characters care about it?
- **Purpose sets the depth.** A settlement can be:
  - local color, sketched in a line;
  - a home base, where the players will fill in some NPCs themselves; it needs a few people who give information, a few merchants and one or two wild cards;
  - an adventure site in its own right.
- **Size profiles.** Rough population, government, defense, commerce and organizations for a village, a town and a city.
- **Atmosphere.** Pick one defining feature and extrapolate the sensory detail from it.
- **Power.** The official ruler is rarely the only power. The book lists many forms of government, from autocracy to theocracy to magocracy, and the noble titles.
- **Flavor.** Merchants carry hooks and rumors. Local coin names add color. Languages and dialects give regions their own speech (pp. 20–21): regional tongues, secret or ancient languages.

### Factions and organizations (pp. 21–23)

- Factions should grow out of the world's existing stories. They give characters patrons, rivals and goals beyond the next adventure.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What role does the faction play in the world, and what does it want?
  - Who founded it, and why?
  - What do its members do, and who are its typical members?
  - What is its symbol, and its motto?
  - How do the characters make contact: agents, headquarters, missions and rewards?
- The book's sample factions follow a consistent shape: overview, motto, beliefs, goals and typical quests.
- **Renown (optional).** A per-faction standing score earned by serving it. It raises members' default attitude, unlocks ranks and perks, and can be lost. **Piety** is the same idea applied to a god.

### Magic in the world (pp. 23–24)

The book poses questions:

- Which magic is ordinary, and which is taboo?
- How rare are casters, and how rare is high-level magic?
- How rare are magic items?
- How do authorities regulate magic?
- How do ordinary folk protect themselves from it?

It suggests restricting or licensing magic and treating magical schools and circles as organizations. It also covers the consequences of travel magic such as teleportation circles, and of resurrection, which needs the soul's consent and is not cheap.

### Creating a campaign in the world (pp. 25–26)

- **Start small.** Prepare three things: a home base; a local region about a day's travel across, with a few adventure sites and one to three other settlements; and a starting adventure. Grow the rest as play needs it.
- **Campaign primer.** Give players a short primer, at most a couple of pages. It covers character options, the history their characters would know, the local area, a few NPCs and some rumors. Everything else is revealed in play.
- **Involve the characters.** Tie their backgrounds to the world. Decide whether they are natives or newcomers. Connect them to the factions driving events. Custom backgrounds can come from the world's own cultures.

### Campaign events (pp. 26–32)

- A world that never changes goes stale. World-shaking events make it feel alive, and change the characters cause matters most.
- **Placement.** Use a few big events per campaign, about three: at the beginning, the middle and the end. Small local events can happen freely. Avoid "false action": an event with no consequence for the characters.
- **Categories of events:**
  - the rise or fall of a leader or an era;
  - cataclysm;
  - invasion;
  - rebellion;
  - extinction or depletion;
  - a new organization;
  - discovery;
  - prophecy;
  - myth and legend.
- Each category comes with prompts to answer. For example: what was always true and now no longer is, who fills the vacuum, and which signs of a prophecy are false.

### Time, endings, style and tone (pp. 32–41)

- **Calendar (pp. 32–33).** Seasons, moons, a holy day per major god, civic festivals and recurring fantastic events.
- **Ending (p. 33).** Close the characters' personal goals. A later campaign in the same world can treat the old party's deeds as legend.
- **Play style (p. 34).** Hack-and-slash or immersive play, and a set of questions about tone: realism, humor, how much planning, maturity and moral ambiguity.
- **Names (p. 36).** Names should sound as if they belong together and to the world.
- **Continuing or episodic (p. 36).** One continuing story, or loosely linked episodes.
- **Theme (p. 36).** A campaign can have an underlying meaning beyond its plot, such as mortality, creeping corruption or the lure of power. It can be varied now and then.
- **Tiers of play (pp. 36–37).** The stakes widen from local to the realm, the nation and the world as characters level. Prepare one tier at a time, and scale world events to match.
- **Flavors of fantasy (pp. 38–41).** Heroic, sword and sorcery, epic, mythic, dark, mystery, intrigue, swashbuckling, war, wuxia, and genre-crossing with science fiction. Each sets different expectations for magic, morality and the heroes' place.

### Cosmology (Ch 2, pp. 43–68)

- **What planes are for (p. 43).** Planes are realms of idea and element around the Material Plane. A usable cosmology needs:
  - homes for fiends, celestials and elementals;
  - a place for the gods;
  - an afterlife;
  - a way to travel between planes;
  - a role for the Astral and Ethereal.
- **Inventing a plane.** Give it one simple, memorable trait that sets a mood.
- **Models (pp. 43–44).** The default Great Wheel, the World Tree, the World Axis and several alternatives: a single cluster of realms, many small planes, planes in orbit, one road linking them, a single mountain of the gods, a sun's path, one world with no outer planes, or one other world.
- **Portals (pp. 44–45).** Portals sit in fitting locations, are often guarded and need a key: a time, a circumstance, a word or an item. Finding the key is itself an adventure.
- **The planes (pp. 46–68).**
  - The Feywild and the Shadowfell are bright and dark echoes of the world. They have crossings and courts, and each has an optional hazard: memory loss and time distortion in the Feywild, despair in the Shadowfell.
  - The Inner Planes are the elemental raw material.
  - The Outer Planes are the realms of the gods, keyed to alignment and stacked in layers, with optional per-plane rules.
  - Between and beyond them sit a neutral plane with a city of doors, demiplanes, and the alien Far Realm.
- **Known worlds (p. 68).** Published settings, each summed up in a sentence or two by one striking condition (gods that abandoned the world, scarce water and metal, a cold war after a great war, and so on). A whole setting can be pitched in one or two sentences.

Most of Ch 2 serves high-tier, planar play. For our solo, low-level Sessions, the usable parts are the cosmology checklist, the one-trait rule for invented planes, portals as keys, and the one-sentence setting pitch.

## What we have now

The glossary also says two contradictory things. **World** says what happens in one Campaign can carry over into another set in the same World. **World memory** says nothing carries over between Campaigns, even in a shared World. The code does the latter.

## Gaps

1. **The Narrator never sees the World.** The premise, genre, magic and tech level are stored but absent from the context that writes every reply. The Arc and first scene see only name and premise. The four enums reach no prompt anywhere.
2. **The World has no lore.** It has no faiths or gods, no powers or factions, no history or fallen past, no magic norms, cultures, languages, naming style or calendar. The book treats these as the core answers a world needs (pp. 9–24). The premise prompt squeezes "lasting powers and tensions" into three sentences.
3. **There is no "what-if" departure.** Nothing names the one way this World differs from the default assumptions (pp. 9–10), the thing that most gives a setting its identity.
4. **There are no factions.** The book makes factions the engine of history and the source of patrons and rivals (pp. 21–23). We have no faction record at World or Campaign level, and extraction cannot record one.
5. **World canon cannot be shared.** Everything about the World that play reveals lives in Campaign-scoped World memory and dies with the Campaign. The World definition promises carry-over (and the book's endings advice assumes it, p. 33), but World memory forbids it.
6. **There is no home base or starting region.** The book's first build step is a home base with a cast and a small region of sites and settlements (pp. 25–26). First Scene makes a single Place and whoever is in it.
7. **Places have no settlement profile.** A Place has no kind or size, ruler or real power, defining sensory feature, or trade (pp. 15–20).
8. **No world-shaking events are planned.** The Arc's Acts have Beats, but nothing plans large changes to the World at the beginning, middle and end (pp. 26–32). `fact.worldChanging` records a change after it happens. It does not plan one.
9. **There is no Campaign theme.** Tone covers mood but not the deeper meaning (p. 36).
10. **There is no player primer or secrets split.** The book separates what the characters already know (a short primer) from what play reveals (pp. 25–26). We have no player-facing "what you know" view. The Journal records what has happened, not what the character starts out knowing.
11. **Characters are not tied to the World.** Character creation doesn't connect backgrounds, bonds or origins to the World's places, factions or faiths (p. 26). The Field AI prompts for backstory and bond know nothing of the World.
12. **There is no cosmology.** `scale = planar` is the only signal, and nothing says what the planes, gods' realms or afterlife are (p. 43).
13. **There is no calendar** of festivals or holy days (pp. 32–33).
14. **There is no standing with powers.** There is no renown with factions or piety with gods (pp. 22–23). Disposition covers individual NPCs only.

## Candidate changes

Ranked by impact on the player's Session first, then by dependency order, then by build cost. The grilling ticket decides which to adopt; this is only a list.

1. **Put the World in the Narrator's fixed context, and the enums in every generation prompt.** Render each linked World's name, premise, genre, scale, magic level and tech level beside the Campaign Seed. Pass the enums to Arc and first-scene generation too. *Rationale:* this is the cheapest change and has the biggest effect, because every reply would finally be consistent with the World the player authored. *ADR:* none. It extends the Narrator context. Check the fixed-context token budget.
2. **Add World lore fields to the Seed card, each with Field AI.** Suggested fields:
   - the faith structure and its major gods or powers (pp. 10–13);
   - magic norms: what is common, taboo or regulated (pp. 23–24);
   - the "what-if" departure from the default assumptions (pp. 9–10);
   - history: a fallen past and the present's scars;
   - a naming style (p. 36).

   All are short, optional text fields, generated per field like the premise. *Rationale:* these are the questions the book says every world must answer, and they feed change 1 directly. *ADR:* none reopened. It follows ADR 0001's per-field pattern, but changes the **Seed** and **Seed card** glossary terms.
3. **Author factions at World creation, and add a faction kind to World memory.** A World faction holds a name, goals, methods, symbol and motto, typical members, and how characters make contact (pp. 21–23). The Narrator and extraction can then refer to factions and record them in play. *Rationale:* factions give a solo player patrons, rivals and reasons to act beyond the current Scene. *ADR:* reopens the **World memory** glossary rule that defers factions. It may touch ADR 0003's extraction scope.
4. **Split World canon from Campaign memory.** Records authored on the World (factions, gods, signature places) become World-scoped and shared by every Campaign in that World. Discoveries in play stay Campaign-scoped unless promoted. *Rationale:* this resolves the contradiction between the **World** and **World memory** terms, and lets a World outlive a Campaign (p. 33). *ADR:* reopens the **World memory** "nothing carries over" rule. The never-delete and supersede rules for Facts would need a World-level equivalent. Do this before change 3 if factions should be World-scoped.
5. **Generate a home base and starting region with the Campaign or first Session.** That means a home settlement with a small cast (people who give information, merchants, a wild card), plus a few nearby sites and one to three other settlements, all as Places joined by exits, plus NPCs (pp. 15–16, 25). First Scene then opens inside it. *Rationale:* the player gets a world to wander from the first Scene instead of one room. *ADR:* none. It changes `first-scene.ts` and the **Place** and **Scene** flow. Benefits from change 6.
6. **Give Place a settlement profile.** Add a kind and size (village, town, city, site), who holds power, and one defining sensory feature to extrapolate from (pp. 15–20). *Rationale:* the Narrator can describe and populate places consistently from a few fields instead of improvising each time. *ADR:* none. It changes the **Place** glossary term and the schema.
7. **Plan world-shaking events on the Arc.** Each Arc plans about three large World events, at the beginning, middle and end Acts, drawn from the book's categories (pp. 26–32). When one fires, it is recorded as a `worldChanging` Fact or a new events kind. *Rationale:* the World visibly changes because of the story and the character's actions, which is the book's cure for stagnation. *ADR:* touches the **Arc**, **Act** and **Fact** glossary terms. An events kind reopens the deferred "events" in **World memory**. Check that it fits the Arc lock and ADR 0005's Exchange flow.
8. **Add a Campaign theme field beside tone.** It carries the underlying meaning (mortality, corruption, the lure of power) for Arc generation and the Narrator (p. 36). *Rationale:* it's a cheap way to make a story feel like it is about something. *ADR:* none. It changes the **Campaign Seed** glossary term.
9. **Tie Character creation to the World.** Pass the World's lore and factions to the Field AI for backstory, bond and custom background. Optionally ask whether the character is a native or a newcomer (p. 26). *Rationale:* a character rooted in the World gives the Narrator hooks from the first Scene. *ADR:* none. It extends the character flow's context the way campaign fields already load Worlds. Depends on change 2.
10. **Add a player primer view.** Show a short, player-facing summary of what the character knows: the World pitch, the home base, notable factions and a few rumors. Keep it separate from secrets only the Narrator sees (pp. 25–26). *Rationale:* it orients a solo player without spoiling anything. *ADR:* none. It sits next to the **Journal**. A "known to the player" flag on World records would need a glossary term.
11. **Add faction renown and god piety.** A standing score per faction or god, kept by code like disposition, shifts members' attitudes (pp. 22–23). *Rationale:* it rewards long play with a faction, but matters little until change 3 exists. *ADR:* new mechanics. It would need a glossary term and possibly an ADR on where standing lives. Renown is not in the SRD 5.1, so it would be our own design.
12. **Add an optional cosmology field** for Worlds with `scale = planar`. It answers the book's checklist (gods' realm, afterlife, fiends and celestials, travel) in a few lines, and gives each invented plane one trait (p. 43). *Rationale:* it helps only planar or high-tier play, so it is low priority now. *ADR:* none. It changes the **Seed** term.
13. **Add a calendar of seasons, holy days and festivals** to the World (pp. 32–33). *Rationale:* it adds texture, but matters little until in-game time is tracked. *ADR:* none, though tracking in-game time may need one.

Out of scope: mapping (scales, hex maps) and the rest of the README "Later" list. Also out: the book's per-plane optional rules and other non-SRD mechanics, which would need their own decision before shipping.
