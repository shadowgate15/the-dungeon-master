# What DMG Ch 1 and Ch 3 teach about Campaigns and Arcs

Date: 2026-11-21. Ticket: #280, under the map #277. Feeds the grilling ticket #284.

Source: the 2014 Dungeon Master's Guide (DMG), the campaign parts of Ch 1 "A World of Your Own" (pp. 25–41) and all of Ch 3 "Creating Adventures" (pp. 71–88). Page numbers are the printed pages. The book is copyrighted, so everything below is a paraphrase in our own words. Nothing is quoted, and no table is reproduced. Where the book gives a random table, this note says only what the table is for.

Our side: `GLOSSARY.md` (Campaign Seed, Tone, Length, Arc, Act, Beat, Beat state, Arc lock), `docs/adr/`, the Arc decisions in #179 and #235, the Campaign field prompts in `src/lib/server/ai/field-registry.ts`, Arc generation in `src/lib/server/arc/generate.ts` and `src/lib/arc/arc.ts`, steering in `src/lib/arc/steering.ts` and `src/lib/server/arc/beat-progress.ts`, and the First Scene in `src/lib/server/session/first-scene.ts`.

## Question

What does the DMG recommend for structuring campaigns and adventures? That covers campaign events, adventure structure and types, hooks, encounters as story units, and complications. How does that compare with our Campaign Seed, Tone, Length, Arc, Act, Beat, Beat state and Arc lock? Which changes are candidates?

## Answer in brief

Our Arc already matches the book's skeleton: a premise with a goal, a threat and a stake, ordered phases, and outcome-shaped Beats that the DM steers toward without scripting. The book adds four ideas that we lack:

1. **A threat with its own agenda.** The book builds stories around an antagonist whose plan moves forward on its own and who reacts when the heroes interfere. Our Arc lists only the outcomes the player's side should reach. Nothing in the world moves unless the player acts. The only push is the drift hook, which offers the player an opportunity.
2. **Adapting the plan.** The book plans in small pieces and expects to rework them as the players surprise it. Under Arc lock, our Arc can only lose Beats (abandoned) after play starts. It can never gain one.
3. **Tying the Character in.** The book wants each character's background tied into the campaign, and it wants space at the end for personal goals. Arc generation never sees the Character.
4. **Complications and outcomes.** The book uses twists, moral quandaries and side quests. It also says every encounter should allow success, partial success or failure, each with consequences. Our Arc has no complication slot. Its resolution is one fixed ending, and a failed Check changes nothing (ADR 0005).

## What the book recommends

### Campaigns (Ch 1)

- **Start small, then grow** (p. 25). You don't plan the whole campaign up front. Run a few adventures and develop the larger plotlines as play goes on. Open the campaign the way you would open an adventure: get into the action quickly, and show the players that adventure is waiting. For the start you only need a home base, the area within about a day's travel, and one first adventure. Detail only what the first sessions need (pp. 25–26).
- **Set the stage** (p. 26). Give the players a short handout, two pages at most, with what their characters would know: any limits on character creation, the backstory the characters would know (which can hint at the campaign's direction), and the starting area with its notable places, known NPCs and rumors of brewing trouble. Hold back everything else, and let the players uncover it in play.
- **Involve the characters** (p. 26). Once you know what the campaign is about, let the players tie their characters' histories to it. Ask simple questions: is the character from here or a newcomer, and are they connected to the people or groups behind the opening events? Say yes where you can, and work the campaign's first threads into each backstory. Backgrounds tied to the world's cultures and organizations root characters most strongly.
- **Campaign events** (pp. 26–32). Big world events are what start and sustain a campaign. The book names inertia as the main risk of open-ended stories: the same villains, the same kind of adventure, and a world that never changes, _especially_ in response to what the characters do. Big events fall at the beginning, middle and end, the way a drama is built. The opening shakes the heroes' world and sets them moving. The middle reverses their fortunes just as they near a goal. The end changes the world for good or ill because of what they did. The book caps it at about three map-changing events per campaign, with as many small local upheavals as you like. It also warns against "false action": spectacle that changes neither the characters nor the world. The book then gives event categories, such as a leader's fall or a disaster. Each comes with prompts for consequences: who gains, who loses, who opposes, and what was not obvious at first. A prophecy gets special treatment. Its omens appear in advance, some of them already fulfilled by play and some still depending on the characters. Factions work to bring it about or stop it, and one part of it is deliberately wrong (p. 32).
- **Tracking time** (pp. 32–33). Keep a calendar, so you can schedule the events that shake up the world, along with festivals and celestial events.
- **Ending a campaign** (p. 33). The ending should tie up the threads of the beginning and middle. Stop when the story reaches its natural end, not at a level cap. Leave room near the end for each character to finish their personal goals; ideally the final adventure fulfills some of them. A later campaign in the same world can show how the old heroes changed it, but the new heroes keep the spotlight.
- **Play style** (p. 34). Agree the style of the game with the players. The two extremes are pure dungeon action and deep roleplay and intrigue. Most games sit between them and mix roleplay and combat. The book's style questions ask, for example, how serious or how gritty the game should be, and how comfortable everyone is with moral ambiguity.
- **Continuing or episodic** (p. 36). A continuing campaign ties its adventures together through a larger purpose or a recurring theme: returning villains, a conspiracy, one mastermind behind everything. It is more work, and the payoff is an epic in which each adventure matters to the next. An episodic campaign runs self-contained stories under one premise, such as hired adventurers.
- **Theme** (p. 36). A theme is the story's deeper meaning, a basic human experience it explores, such as facing mortality. It is not a villain or a plot. One theme can carry many unconnected adventures. Change things up now and then (variations on a theme), ideally with a detour that turns out to connect to the main plot.
- **Tiers of play** (pp. 36–38). Stakes grow with the characters' power: first a village, then a region, then a realm, then the world. The tiers are the natural points to bring in the next world-shaking event, as each resolved threat gives way to a new or transformed one. Design one tier at a time. You then only detail what comes next, and the players' surprises cost less rework.
- **Factions** (pp. 21–22). Organizations give characters patrons, allies and enemies beyond single NPCs. Villainous organizations create an ongoing sense of menace. A faction should grow out of the stories that matter to the world.
- **Flavors of fantasy** (pp. 38–41). The book surveys sub-genres such as heroic, sword and sorcery, epic, mythic, dark, intrigue, mystery, swashbuckling, war and wuxia. For us these belong to a World's genre flavor and a Campaign's Tone, not to the Arc.

### Adventures (Ch 3)

- **An adventure is an episode, and a campaign is the series** (p. 71). It should read as a story, not a run of disconnected encounters.
- **What great adventures share** (pp. 71–72):
  - a **credible threat** whose goals the heroes can discover and thwart;
  - **familiar tropes with a twist**;
  - **focus on the present**: just enough history to start the story, with the attention on what the antagonists are doing now and how the heroes get drawn in;
  - **heroes who matter**: more than one possible outcome, so the players never feel railroaded (if the big villain appears early, the heroes must be able to beat it then);
  - **something for every player**: a mix of exploration, social interaction and combat, tuned to the actual table;
  - **occasional surprises**: discoveries, unexpected guests, red herrings, used sparingly.
- **Structure** (p. 72):
  - The **beginning** has a hook that gives the characters a compelling reason to get involved. It is exciting and focused, and it gives a sense of where the story is going and something to look forward to.
  - In the **middle**, each challenge brings a choice with a visible effect on the ending. Discovered secrets can change the heroes' goal or their understanding of events. Meanwhile the antagonists pursue their plan, cover their tracks and strike at meddlers. The heroes must never become spectators.
  - The **ending** holds the climax, which must hinge on the characters' choices and never be decided in advance. It doesn't have to tie up everything. A loose thread leads into the next adventure.
- **Location-based adventures** (pp. 72–75): set the party's goal, identify the key NPCs (villain, allies, patron, any of whom may be a villain in disguise), detail the place, choose an introduction that grows naturally from the goal and the setting, choose a climax, and key the encounters to the map. The tables give ideas for each step.
- **Event-based adventures** (pp. 75–77) suit our improvised, map-less play best. The steps:
  1. **Start with a villain.**
  2. **Write the villain's timeline.** It is what the villain would do if nobody interfered. The book offers a few patterns for how the plan unfolds, such as building toward a fixed date or escalating over time.
  3. **Set the party's goal.**
  4. **Identify the NPCs.** Most of them hold undecided attitudes until the heroes meet them.
  5. **Anticipate the villain's reactions.** For each step of the plan, ask what happens if the heroes stop it and how the villain compensates. A flowchart can track this.
  6. **Detail only the key locations.**
  7. **Choose an introduction and a climax.**
  8. **List possible encounters.** They happen wherever the villain's agenda crosses the heroes' path.
- **Mysteries** (pp. 77–78) add a victim, suspects and clues. A victim linked to the villain, or to the heroes, carries emotional weight. A closed circle of suspects works well, and several suspects should hide unrelated secrets. Clues point to motive, means or opportunity, and some point the wrong way. **Plant redundant clues**, because players will miss some, and a mystery that is too easy beats one that frustrates.
- **Intrigue** (p. 78) is event-based play about power and favors. It can have no villain or many, each with an agenda, where foiling one may advance another. The book suggests tracking the party's **influence** with factions or NPCs. It only works if the players care about the outcome, which usually means it serves their own goals.
- **Framing events** (p. 79). A scheduled public occasion, such as a festival, can frame an adventure or serve as its hook.
- **Complications** (pp. 79–81):
  - **Moral quandaries**: a single, hard choice that no spell or sword can settle, such as saving innocents or catching the villain. If a character breaks an oath, give them a way to atone.
  - **Twists**: something that makes the goal harder to reach, such as a time limit or a rival after the same prize.
  - **Side quests**: off the main path, but completing one helps the main goal.
- **Encounters as story units** (p. 81). An encounter is a scene in the larger story. It should be fun for the players and easy for the DM to run. It needs a clear objective and a connection to the campaign's story: it builds on what came before and foreshadows what comes next. **Every encounter can end in success, partial success or failure, and each outcome must have consequences**, so the players' choices matter. Make the objective clear to the players, or they drift into boredom. Players who set their own objectives should be encouraged; players who ignore the objective face the consequences. The book then gives a few objective archetypes, such as protecting someone or stopping a ritual, each paired with a typical complication.
- **Combat difficulty, the adventuring day and random encounters** (pp. 81–87) are out of scope for this map. One idea carries over: random encounters should build on the session's story, not compete with it. They set the atmosphere, reinforce campaign themes, foreshadow and add urgency. The book suggests using them when players stall, rest, travel long distances or draw attention to themselves (pp. 85–86).

## Gaps

1. **No antagonist and no agenda that moves on its own.** The book's main structural tool for improvised play is a villain with a timeline and planned reactions (pp. 75–77). Our Arc names stakes, but no one pursues them. Every Beat is phrased as the protagonists' outcome. The drift hook can only offer opportunities. It can never make the threat advance, so a passive player meets a static world. The book names that as the cause of stagnation (p. 26).
2. **The Arc cannot adapt after the first Session.** The book plans in pieces and reworks as players surprise it (pp. 25, 37), and it expects villains to adapt (p. 77). Arc lock and #179 allow only reaching and abandoning. A player who derails Act 2 abandons Beats and leaves the Arc with nothing to steer toward. The Arc then finishes early or hollow.
3. **The Character is not part of the story plan.** The book ties backgrounds to the campaign (p. 26) and saves space at the end for personal goals (p. 33). Arc generation takes no Character input. The First Scene gets only name, race and class. The personality trait, ideal, bond and flaw are never used to shape the story.
4. **A fixed ending against "never a foregone conclusion".** The book says the climax must hinge on the heroes and allow more than one outcome (pp. 71–72). Our resolution is a single "intended ending", and the Narrator is told to build toward it.
5. **No complications in the plan.** Twists, moral quandaries, reversals and side quests (pp. 79–81) have no place in the Arc. All Beats look alike. The middle of the story, which the book describes as discovered secrets that change the goal (p. 72), is not asked for.
6. **The structure isn't anchored to dramatic turns.** The book's beginning, middle and end shocks (p. 27) map well onto our Acts, but the Act prompt asks only for "a name and purpose, such as setup, escalation or climax". Nothing asks for an inciting event, a midpoint reversal, or a climax that changes the world.
7. **No key cast.** The book identifies the villain, allies and patron before play (pp. 74, 77). Our NPCs appear only in the First Scene and in play, so the Arc's threat has no named face for the Narrator to keep consistent.
8. **Mystery has no clue structure.** Tone has "mysterious", and premises often describe mysteries. The book says a mystery needs suspects and redundant clues (pp. 77–78). Without a hidden list of secrets and clues, the Narrator improvises each one, and the player may find nothing, or contradictions.
9. **Failure changes nothing.** Encounters should allow success, partial success or failure, each with consequences (p. 81). ADR 0005 leaves failed Checks with no state change. This is mostly a Running play topic, but it also limits whether Beats can be "failed into".
10. **Theme is missing.** The book separates theme, a story's deeper meaning (p. 36), from tone. We have only Tone, and the glossary rules out "theme" as a synonym for it, so neither the Arc nor the Narrator gets a throughline to echo.
11. **No player-facing primer.** The book's short handout of what the characters know (p. 26) has no equivalent. The player starts a Session with only the Campaign premise they wrote or generated.
12. **Long campaigns have one finite outline.** The book plans a long campaign tier by tier, each tier with a new or transformed threat (pp. 36–37), and three major events across the whole (p. 27). Length "long" is defined as an open-ended saga, yet its Arc is one outline of 4–5 Acts, and the Arc ends when no Beat is pending.
13. **No sense of time or scheduled events.** Calendars, framing events and a villain's timeline (pp. 32, 75, 79) all assume an in-world clock. We have none, only Exchange counts.
14. **Smaller prompt-level gaps.** Stakes are not scaled to Length (the tiers idea, p. 36). There is no nudge toward "focus on the present" in the Arc premise. There is no guidance to end each Act with a loose thread that leads into the next (p. 72). And nothing marks the campaign as continuing or episodic (p. 36).

## Candidate changes

Roughly ranked by the map's rule: impact on a Session first, then dependencies, then build cost. #284 decides which to adopt. "Reopens" names the ADR or the earlier decision a change would revisit. Arc decisions live in #179, #235 and `GLOSSARY.md`, not in an ADR.

1. **Add an antagonist agenda to the Arc.** Give the Arc a named threat with a goal and an ordered list of steps it takes if nobody interferes (a "threat track"). Rationale: this is the book's main engine for event-based play, and it gives steering something to advance besides the player (gaps 1 and 7). Reopens: the Arc's parts (#179), the glossary entries for Arc and Beat ("what should become true" for the protagonists only). No ADR.
2. **Let the threat advance when play stalls.** When the drift hook fires, or at set points, the Narrator is told to make the threat's next step happen on screen, as well as offering an opportunity. Code advances the step, the same way it changes Beat state. Rationale: this fixes the static world without scripting the player (gap 1). It depends on 1. Reopens: the drift design in #235. No ADR.
3. **Feed the Character into Arc generation.** Pass the linked Character's background, personality trait, ideal, bond and flaw to the skeleton call, and ask for at least one Beat that pays off a personal thread, placed in the last Act. Rationale: the book's "involve the characters" and "finish personal goals" (gap 3). It is cheap, but it needs a rule for when the Character changes after the Arc is generated. Reopens: the Arc-before-Character order and Arc lock, which today lock the Character separately. No ADR.
4. **Allow Beats to be added after the Arc is locked.** When Beats in the current or next Act are abandoned, a silent call proposes replacement Beats (up to the Act's size cap), validated by code and appended as pending. Accepted Beats are never edited. Rationale: the book plans in pieces and adapts, and a derailed Arc otherwise goes hollow (gap 2). Reopens: Arc lock, #179 ("no mid-Campaign rewrites"), the glossary entry for Beat state. No ADR. This one may need its own map.
5. **Replace the single resolution with open outcomes.** Swap "resolution" for the climax's dramatic question plus what the world looks like if the heroes succeed and if they fail (optionally a partial result). Rationale: "never a foregone conclusion" (gap 4). It is a cheap prompt and schema change. Reopens: the Arc's parts (#179), the steering text in `steering.ts`. No ADR.
6. **Anchor the Acts to dramatic turns.** Have the skeleton prompt give the first Act an inciting event, a middle Act a reversal or discovered secret that changes the goal, and the last Act a climax that changes the world. Ask each non-final Act to end on a thread that leads into the next. Rationale: the book's beginning, middle and end structure (gaps 5, 6 and 14). Prompt only. Reopens nothing.
7. **Add a complication per Act.** Give each Act an optional complication (a twist or a moral quandary) or a Beat kind (turning point, reveal, quandary, climax). Rationale: it varies play and gives the middle real choices (gap 5). It is cheap as a prompt change and moderate as a schema field. Reopens: the Beat shape (#179), if it is a field.
8. **Generate a key cast with the Arc.** Write a short cast of the villain, a patron or ally, and one ambiguous figure into World memory as NPCs not yet introduced, so the Narrator keeps them consistent. Rationale: the book's "identify important NPCs" (gap 7). It depends on 1. Reopens: the glossary entries for NPC and Journal (an NPC is "one the DM has introduced", and the Journal shows only met NPCs), and World memory provenance (records carry the Session that created them). No ADR, but it touches World memory decisions.
9. **Add a hidden secrets-and-clues list for mysteries.** For mysterious-Tone or mystery-premise Campaigns, generate per Act a few secrets, each reachable by at least two clues. The Narrator sees them, and the Beat check can use them. Rationale: the book's redundant clues (gap 8). Rather than a separate store, consider modeling them as unrevealed World memory Facts. Reopens: the Fact concept (facts are "established" by the story). No ADR. May need its own map.
10. **Make a failed Check cost something.** Let a failed Check carry a setback or complication Effect: the threat advances, or something is lost, revealed or delayed. Rationale: the three-outcome encounter rule (gap 9), and it pairs with 2. It belongs to the Running play area. Reopens: **ADR 0005** (failed Checks change no state; fail forward deferred).
11. **Add an optional Theme to the Campaign Seed.** A short free-text field with a Field AI control, passed to the Arc and the Narrator. Rationale: gives a throughline separate from mood (gap 10). Reopens: the glossary entries for Tone (`_Avoid_: theme`) and Campaign Seed. ADR 0001 already covers adding a field this way.
12. **Show a player primer at the first Session.** A short, generated "what your Character knows" (the starting place, known figures, rumors), drawn from the Campaign premise, Act 1 and the First Scene. It is shown once and never reveals Beats. Rationale: the book's campaign handout (gap 11). It is cheap and visible. Reopens nothing, but it must respect "never announce beats".
13. **Chain Arcs for long Campaigns.** When an Arc completes, offer to generate the next Arc from the old one's outcome, using reached and abandoned Beats and World memory, with raised stakes. Rationale: the tier-by-tier planning, and "long" being open-ended (gap 12). Reopens: "a Campaign has at most one Arc", Arc lock, #179. This is map-sized.
14. **Scale stakes and focus by Length in the prompts.** One-shot and short stay local, and long may escalate to realm or world by its final Act. The Arc premise should be about the present situation, not history. Rationale: tiers and focus on the present (gap 14). Prompt only. Reopens nothing.
15. **Add a continuing or episodic switch.** Let a Campaign choose a chain of self-contained Arcs with one premise rather than a single Arc. Rationale: the book's episodic campaigns (p. 36). It depends on 13. It is low priority for solo play. Reopens: the Arc model (#179).
16. **Add an in-world clock and scheduled events.** Track days, so that framing events and a threat timeline can be dated. Rationale: calendars and framing events (gap 13). It is a large build with no player-visible payoff until 1 and 2 exist. Reopens nothing directly. It probably belongs to the Running play or Worlds areas.

Out of scope here, but noted for the map: factions and influence tracking (pp. 21–22 and 78) belong to the World memory area. Random encounters, combat difficulty and the adventuring day belong to a future combat effort.
